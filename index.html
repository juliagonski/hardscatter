The Science

Closing a proof is one thing. Real data is another.

Lately the headlines belong to AI systems cracking famous theorems and closed-form results: clean statements, exact answers, no noise. Experimental science doesn't hand you that. It hands you detector noise, systematic uncertainties, and data that argues back.

At the Large Hadron Collider, protons are accelerated to nearly the speed of light and smashed together 40 million times a second. Each collision sprays hundreds of particles through house-sized detectors, which record the debris as it curves through magnetic fields and dumps energy into layered calorimeters.

Out of that chaos, physicists reconstruct what actually happened: the tracks, the jets, the rare signatures that might point to new physics. It's pattern-finding at a scale and messiness no whiteboard proof prepares you for, and it's exactly the kind of challenge our environments are built from.

Our approach, laid out in the research.

arXiv:2611.15885

Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning

Alexander Yue · Qibin Liu · Julia Gonski

We turn hard, still-unsolved high-energy physics problems into reinforcement-learning environments with automatic, continuously scored rewards. Frontier reasoning models still struggle with these tasks, so we demonstrate a proof of concept where a small language model teaches itself to do better through RL, with no human in the loop. It's a first step from AI that mimics known physics workflows toward agents that autonomously discover better science.
